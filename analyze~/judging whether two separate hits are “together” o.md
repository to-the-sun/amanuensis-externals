# judging whether two separate hits are “together” or “offset” in music, where perceptual fusion can occur up to ~30–50 ms.

You’re right to focus on that range—when judging whether two separate hits (like drumbeats) are “together” or “offset” in music, the perceptual boundary is much looser than the ~2–5 ms gap-detection threshold. Here’s why ~30–50 ms is commonly cited:

## Perceptual fusion vs. gap detection

Human auditory grouping treats two onsets as a single event if they fall within a certain window. This is distinct from detecting a tiny silent gap in a continuous sound:

- **Gap detection**: ~1.5–3 ms for similar clicks, up to ~10 ms for dissimilar sounds.[^1]
- **Onset-order / “different start times” judgment**: If two overlapping sounds have different onsets, they are heard as starting at different times only when their onsets differ by **more than about 20 ms** (classic work by Hirsh; Hirsch \& Sherrick).[^1]
- **Haas / precedence effect (spatial fusion)**: When the *same* sound arrives from two directions, delays up to **~30 ms** (sometimes up to **40 ms**) are still perceived as a single event, with direction determined by the first arrival.[^2][^3]

In music production and psychoacoustics, this fusion window is often generalized to **~30–50 ms** for complex, real-world sounds: within that window, two hits tend to be heard as a single, slightly thickened or “flammed” event rather than clearly separate beats.[^2]

## Why the window is so large in music

Several factors push the effective “together” window up from a few milliseconds to tens of milliseconds:

- **Attack shape and duration**: Real drum hits have finite rise times and decays; if the second hit lands during the first hit’s attack/early decay, the auditory system often fuses them.[^4][^1]
- **Spectral similarity**: Similar timbres and overlapping frequency content increase fusion; dissimilar sounds are easier to separate even at smaller offsets.[^5][^1]
- **Context and expectation**: In rhythmic contexts, the brain uses predictive timing; small deviations are interpreted as expressive timing rather than distinct events.[^6][^7]
- **Task**: Judging “are these two hits together?” in a mix is different from “can you detect a 2 ms gap in noise.” The former tolerates larger offsets before you say “those are two hits.”[^8][^1][^2]


## Practical mapping for drum hits

Roughly, for two drum transients of similar timbre:

- **0–10 ms**: Almost always fused; perceived as a single, slightly thicker hit (or a very tight flam).[^1][^2]
- **10–20 ms**: Often still fused, but may start to sound like a subtle flam or double hit depending on context.[^2][^1]
- **20–30 ms**: Transition zone: some listeners hear two events, others hear one smeared hit; highly context-dependent.[^1][^2]
- **30–50 ms**: Increasingly heard as two distinct hits, though still possibly grouped as a single musical gesture (e.g., a flam or grace note).[^3][^2]
- **> 50 ms**: Clearly two separate events in most musical contexts.[^6][^2]

So the “perceptual fusion up to ~30–50 ms” statement applies to the *musical* judgment of togetherness in complex, overlapping transients, not the absolute physical limit of temporal resolution.[^3][^2][^1]
